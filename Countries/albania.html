Albania

Travel Guide & Archive

The Land of the Eagles: Ottoman Stone Towns, Canyons, and Mountain Citadels

Documenting the raw transitions of the Balkans—where vibrant, bunker-filled capitals lead to hidden emerald canyons, historic stone citadels, and dramatic mountain strongholds.

Advertisement

Nestled along the shifting geopolitical and natural borders of the Balkan Peninsula, Albania stands out as one of Europe’s most enigmatic and rewarding chapters. Holding a proud place in this 150-country archive, it reveals an immense wealth of preserved historical landmarks, wild un-dammed river networks, and a deep, complex modern history. Moving from the hyper-colored central valleys up to the isolated highlands, this file outlines a journey across a landscape completely untamed by mass commercialism.

Tirana: The Color-Blocked Capital

The journey kicks off in Tirana, a city that beats with an electric, chaotic energy designed to break historical molds. Once defined by grey, austere block architecture, the capital has been famously transformed by vibrant, multi-colored paint schemes splashed across its buildings. Centered around the massive open expanse of Skanderbeg Square, Tirana showcases its dark communist past through BUNK'ART—massive underground concrete military shelters converted into haunting historical exhibits. At street level, the upscale Blloku district moves to a pulse of thriving open-air cafes, serving strong espresso and flaky local Byrek pastries filled with wild greens and salty curd cheese.

Gramsh: Canyons and Hidden Waters

Venturing inland away from the primary transit routes brings you to the rugged, industrial-turned-ecological enclave of Gramsh. Flanked by dramatic valleys and the winding course of the Devoll River, this region is a paradise for raw, off-the-beaten-path terrain tracking. The real gem near Gramsh is the spectacular Sotira Waterfall, cascading down the sheer limestone rock face of Mount Tomorr. Navigating the surrounding canyons and technical unpaved trails requires real effort, revealing turquoise pools and dramatic geological cliffs completely untouched by major tourism infrastructures.

Gjirokaster: The Stone City

Traveling south into the heart of historical Epirus leads to the UNESCO-listed stone stronghold of Gjirokaster. Built entirely of local silver-grey slate, this remarkably preserved Ottoman town clings steeply to the hillside beneath a massive medieval fortress. Walking the steep, polished limestone cobblestones of the Old Bazaar reveals imposing fortified tower houses (Kullas) that look like tiny castles. The town is a sensory gateway into old Balkan folklore; days in Gjirokaster are soundtracked by polyphonic singing and fueled by local specialties like Qifqi—savory, pan-fried rice balls seasoned with fresh mint and local eggs.

Kruje: The Mountain Citadel

Perched high on a dramatic cliffside overlooking the valleys below sits the historic mountain stronghold of Kruje. This iconic site represents the defiant heart of Albanian history, where the legendary commander Skanderbeg held off the Ottoman Empire from his vertical fortress walls. The mountain photography here is exceptional, capturing sharp panoramic views of the rugged landscape dropping away into the Adriatic plains. Walking through the restored medieval wooden corridors of the Old Bazaar reveals a dense concentration of local craftsmen selling hand-woven textiles and copper pieces, keeping old ancestral traditions alive beneath the towering limestone peaks.
