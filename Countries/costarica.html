Pura Vida and the Pacific Canopy

Touching down in the central valleys of San José and Alajuela, before pushing west to the coastal hub of Quepos to witness the immense biodiversity of Manuel Antonio.

Advertisement

Costa Rica is a country almost entirely defined by its staggering, concentrated biodiversity. Documenting the dense rainforests required completely rethinking the camera setup. The heavy Canon with a long telephoto lens was permanently slung over my shoulder to catch the incredibly subtle movements high in the canopy, while a macro lens became absolutely essential for night hikes when the jungle floor comes alive with vibrant, tiny amphibians.

The Central Hubs: San José and Alajuela

The expedition kicked off in the bustling capital of San José, a sprawling urban center ringed by green mountains. It serves as the cultural and logistical heart of the country. Just to the northwest lies the neighboring city of Alajuela, a slightly more relaxed, historic town that effectively operates as the main gateway for incoming travelers. Spending a day navigating the lively central markets of both cities was the perfect way to acclimatize to the local "Pura Vida" rhythm before heading into the wild.

The Coastal Edge: Quepos and Manuel Antonio

Trading the cooler central valley for the heavy humidity of the Pacific coast, the basecamp shifted to the vibrant harbor town of Quepos. This town is the launchpad for one of the country's undisputed crown jewels: Manuel Antonio National Park. It is a stunning, concentrated slice of nature where the dense rainforest crashes directly into perfect white-sand beaches, creating an environment where wildlife thrives right on the edge of the ocean.

Sloths, Monkeys, and Tree Frogs

The true highlight of Manuel Antonio is the sheer density of the animals. Trekking the humid, shaded trails required keeping your eyes permanently trained on the branches above. It was a photographer's dream: managing to spot the incredibly well-camouflaged sloths slowly navigating the high canopy, while chaotic, highly energetic troops of squirrel monkeys and white-faced capuchins swung through the lower branches (often scheming to steal snacks from unsuspecting beachgoers). As the sun went down and the jungle transitioned, switching to a macro setup allowed me to capture the brilliantly vibrant, iconic red-eyed tree frogs clinging to the damp foliage—an absolute staple of the Costa Rican wild.

Gallo Pinto and the Casado

Costa Rican food is deeply comforting and relentlessly hearty, meant to fuel long days of trekking. The morning routine strictly revolved around a massive plate of Gallo Pinto—a deeply savory mix of rice and black beans stir-fried with onions, bell peppers, and cilantro, usually served alongside scrambled eggs and sweet fried plantains. For lunch after sweating through the jungle trails, you sit down for a traditional Casado, a perfectly balanced "married" plate featuring rice, beans, a fresh salad, plantains, and your choice of beautifully grilled chicken or fish caught right off the coast of Quepos.
