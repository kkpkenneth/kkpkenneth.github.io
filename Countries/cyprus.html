Divided Capitals and Sun-Drenched Coasts

Exploring the divided streets of Nicosia, the ancient ruins of Paphos, the lively beaches of Ayia Napa, the coastal charm of Larnaca, and crossing the line to the historic, haunting city of Famagusta.

Advertisement

Cyprus is a complex, sun-baked island located at the historic crossroads of Europe and the Middle East. It offers a jarring but fascinating mix of sprawling ancient ruins, pristine party beaches, and modern geopolitical tension. Documenting this contrast required keeping the gear versatile: the wide-angle Canon was perfect for capturing the scale of the coastal amphitheaters, the Sony excelled in the dimly lit, vibrant tavernas at night, and the Xiaomi was kept easily accessible for quick, discreet photos when navigating near the heavily patrolled UN buffer zones.

The Divide: Nicosia and Famagusta

The journey must include Nicosia, the last divided capital in the world. Walking down Ledra Street, the bustling pedestrian thoroughfare abruptly ends at the "Green Line," a UN-patrolled buffer zone separating the Republic of Cyprus from Northern Cyprus. Crossing the checkpoint feels like stepping into a different country altogether. Further east along the coast lies Famagusta. Beyond its incredible walled city and Gothic architecture, it is most famous for Varosha—a former luxury resort district that was completely abandoned during the 1974 conflict, leaving a haunting, crumbling ghost town frozen in time right on the edge of the Mediterranean.

The Eastern Shores: Larnaca and Ayia Napa

Back in the south, the coastal vibe takes over completely. Larnaca offers a wonderful, relaxed introduction to the island with its palm-lined Finikoudes promenade and historic salt lakes. Just a short drive away is Ayia Napa, globally famous for its relentless, high-energy nightlife. But beyond the clubs, it genuinely boasts some of the most spectacular, crystalline waters and white-sand beaches on the entire island, with spots like Nissi Beach drawing crowds looking to soak up the Mediterranean sun.

Myth and Mosaics: Paphos

Heading to the far western edge of the island brings you to Paphos, a city so rich in history that the entire town is listed as a UNESCO World Heritage site. It is steeped in mythology as the legendary birthplace of Aphrodite. Spending a day wandering through the sprawling Kato Paphos Archaeological Park reveals some of the most intricate, well-preserved Roman floor mosaics in the world, while exploring the subterranean, solid-rock Tombs of the Kings offers a stark, monumental look into the island's ancient past.

Halloumi, Souvlaki, and Commandaria

Cypriot cuisine is a brilliant, heavy-hitting fusion of Greek and Middle Eastern flavors. You cannot spend a day here without eating Halloumi; this dense, salty, squeaky cheese is indigenous to the island and is usually served thick-cut and perfectly grilled over charcoal. For a quick meal, grabbing a warm pita absolutely stuffed with charcoal-grilled pork or chicken Souvlaki, fresh tomatoes, and tzatziki is mandatory. To wash it all down, finishing a long, rich meze dinner with a glass of Commandaria—an incredibly sweet, amber-colored dessert wine that holds the title of the world's oldest named wine still in production—is the perfect nod to the island's deep history.
