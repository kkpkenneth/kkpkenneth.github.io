From the Silicon Delta to the Silk Road

A lifelong timeline spanning childhood memories in Beijing, experiencing the World Expo in Shanghai, working in the hyper-modern hubs of Guangdong and Shenzhen, and pushing into the deep interior across Xi'an, Wuhan, Yinchuan, Xining, Urumqi, Hohhot, and the border town of Erenhot.

Mainland China is less of a single country and more of a shifting, rapidly accelerating universe. My relationship with it has evolved through distinct eras of my life. Capturing this scale meant constantly adapting: the Canon captured the sheer, ancient magnitude of the capital when I was younger, while years later, the Xiaomi was an absolute necessity for snapping quick photos and scanning mobile payments in the incredibly fast-paced, cashless tech hubs of the south.

Imperial Echoes and Future Visions: Beijing and Shanghai

My earliest memories of travel are rooted in Beijing as a kid. The physical toll of climbing the endless, uneven stone steps of the Great Wall, the sheer scale of the courtyards within the Forbidden City, and standing before the perfect circular symmetry of Tian Tan (The Temple of Heaven) left a permanent imprint. Decades later, witnessing the Shanghai World Expo offered a completely flipped perspective: trading ancient stone for sweeping, futuristic pavilions along the Huangpu River, showcasing a country rapidly announcing its place on the modern global stage.

The Engine Room: Working in Guangdong and Shenzhen

Living and working in Guangdong provided a front-row seat to the country's economic heartbeat. Shenzhen, in particular, operates at a frequency that is hard to describe until you live it. Going from a humble fishing village to a global tech megalopolis in a few decades, it is a city of towering skyscrapers, massive electronic markets, and an intense, relentless hustle. Navigating the daily grind here offers a profound look at modern, urban Chinese life that a simple vacation rarely uncovers.

The Deep Interior and the Northern Frontiers

Beyond the coastal megacities lies an entirely different world. The journey through the interior traced ancient history through the terracotta warriors in Xi'an and the massive, sprawling river energy of Wuhan. Pushing west and north, the cultural shifts become staggering. From the Islamic architecture and Hui culture of Yinchuan to the high-altitude, Tibetan plateau gateway of Xining, and the vibrant Uyghur bazaars of Urumqi in Xinjiang, the diversity is immense. Heading up into Inner Mongolia, the vast grasslands surrounding Hohhot eventually funnel into the fascinating, dinosaur-flanked border outpost of Erenhot, where the trains cross out into the Gobi.

Dim Sum, Peking Duck, and Yangrou Chuan

The culinary landscape of China changes drastically across its provinces. Working in the south meant being completely spoiled by authentic Dim Sum—spending weekend mornings navigating towers of bamboo steamers filled with hargow (shrimp dumplings) and perfectly sweet, baked pork buns. Up north, the crispy, sugar-dipped skin of authentic Peking Duck in the capital remains an unforgettable childhood core memory. And out in the vast western frontiers, absolutely nothing beats ending a long, dusty travel day with a fistful of fiercely spiced, cumin-dusted Yangrou Chuan (lamb skewers) roasted over an open street-side charcoal grill.
